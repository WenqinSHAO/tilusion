The Clockmaker’s Orchard

Chapter 2 · The Brass Observatory

The rain had stopped by the time Mara Vell reached the orchard gate, but the brass leaves still chimed as though the storm were passing through them. Beneath the arch, a small plate had been polished clean: OBSERVATORY CLOSED UNTIL THE SECOND MOON.

She found Elian crouched beside the western wall, turning a cracked compass in his palm. “It points below,” he said, and for once he did not smile. The needle tugged toward the roots of the oldest tree.

In the glasshouse, every clock had stopped at the third bell. Mara wrote this in the margin of her field notebook, underlining it twice, then added a question: why did the pendulums stop before the bell was rung?

A lantern moved on the upper balcony. The figure holding it wore the gray sleeves of the Royal Archivist, though no archivist was meant to be in the orchard before dawn.

“We should leave,” Elian whispered. But Mara had already seen the chalk line across the floor. It circled the central telescope, then broke where someone had dragged a box through it.

Inside the box, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the red needle from the city clock. Its surface was warm. The orchard bells answered it with one clear note, and somewhere below them a lock opened.

The Archivist saw them then. “Do not touch the mechanism,” she called down. Her voice echoed from too many directions, as if she had spoken from the balcony and from the stairwell and from the roots at once.

Mara lifted both hands. Elian did not. He slipped the compass into his coat and stepped backward until his heel found the hidden seam in the floorboards.

The seam widened. Cold air rose through it, smelling of iron, apples, and extinguished candles. Below the glasshouse was a stairway that had not appeared on any map of Lyr.

“Your father knew about this,” the Archivist said. Mara stopped moving. Elian looked at her with the expression he used when he had been keeping a secret for too long.

The bells rang a second time, but this time only Mara seemed to hear them. The red needle turned by itself toward the dark stair. In her notebook, the ink from the third-bell question spread into a black circle.

She stepped through the seam first. Behind her, the glasshouse clocks began again—not together, but one after another, as if each had remembered a different version of the hour.
